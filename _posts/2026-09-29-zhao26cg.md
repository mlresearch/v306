abstract: Synthetic data has become a common component of machine learning research.
  While widely adopted, its use in privacy-sensitive contexts has quietly shifted
  from a claim of residual inference risk under stated assumptions to an appearance-based
  property inferred from data generation itself. In this position paper, we argue
  that this shift reflects an implicit change in community standards for what counts
  as sufficient privacy evidence, rather than a misunderstanding of well-established
  privacy principles. Drawing on an empirical analysis of recent publications across
  major ML venues, we show that synthetic data is frequently used in privacy-sensitive
  settings without explicit articulation of threat models, inference risks, or falsifiable
  privacy claims. As a result, privacy assurance often remains implicit, difficult
  to verify, and unevenly distributed, with heightened exposure for rare and minority
  records. We argue for treating privacy as an explicit, evidence-based scientific
  claim and recommend that ML venues adopt norms requiring privacy-relevant assertions
  to be clearly scoped, testable, and contestable.
