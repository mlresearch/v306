abstract: 'Large language models are useful because we taught them to give us what
  we want. That works when success can be judged immediately, but people increasingly
  bring these systems their relationships, their hard decisions, and their long-term
  goals, where what a user wants to hear and what serves them best are frequently
  different. We argue that LLM providers should offer at least one widely accessible,
  opt-in mode optimized and evaluated for long-term well-being rather than next-turn
  approval. This is a pressing need, as models have been found to endorse questionable
  framings well above human baselines, users take AI advice readily without their
  well-being improving, and sycophantic models raise dependence while lowering prosocial
  intent. We propose three principles—change the objective, give users explicit relational
  roles, avoid paternalism—and organize the design space around three choices the
  current paradigm makes implicitly: the horizon over which well-being is measured
  (When), whose interests it represents (Who), and what role the assistant plays (How).'
